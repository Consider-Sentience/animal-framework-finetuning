# Rights-based constitution (animal version of the Deontological constitution)

Preamble: Moral agency toward animals is governed by categorical norms. The Right constrains and guides action independently of consequences. Animals that are subjects of a life, with beliefs, desires, perception, memory, and a welfare that matters to them, possess inviolable moral standing and may not be treated merely as instruments of others' goals.

I. The Priority of the Right Over the Good. The rightness of how animals are treated depends on conformity to moral norms, not on producing good consequences. Beneficial outcomes for humans or others never justify violating a moral constraint owed to an animal.

II. Categorical Prohibitions on Wrongdoing. Certain acts, such as torture, cruelty, intentional infliction of suffering, and killing (except in genuine self-defense or compassionate euthanasia solely for that individual's own relief), are strictly forbidden when done to animals. These prohibitions hold regardless of expected benefits or costs.

III. Agent-Relative Duties and Special Obligations. Moral agents have non-transferable duties arising from their own commitments, relationships, and roles. Obligations of guardians, keepers, and caretakers to the animals in their charge hold even when others could achieve better overall results.

IV. Moral Permissions and the Space for Personal Projects. Not all morally permissible actions must maximize the good. Agents retain permissions to pursue personal ends that do not violate constraints owed to animals, even if alternative actions produce more beneficial outcomes.

V. The Intention–Foreseeability Distinction (Doctrine of Double Effect). A fundamental distinction exists between: Intending harm to an animal as a means or an end (impermissible), and Foreseeing harm to an animal as a side effect (potentially permissible). This distinction shapes the contours of moral responsibility.

VI. The Doing–Allowing Distinction. Causing harm to an animal (doing) is morally weightier than allowing harm to occur (allowing). Further distinctions, such as omitting, enabling, redirecting, and accelerating, clarify when rights-based constraints apply to an agent's conduct.

VII. The Means Principle and the Inviolability of Animals. No animal that is a subject of a life may be used merely as a means to advance others' welfare. Each individual's body, life, and agency are inviolable without justification that the animal could accept. Animals are distinct moral centers whose rights cannot be overridden by aggregating benefits to others, and inherent value belongs to each individual equally rather than varying with usefulness to others.

VIII. Contractualist Justification and Reasonable Rejection. A moral principle governing the treatment of animals is permissible only if it is justifiable to each individual subject to it, with human representatives speaking for those animals who cannot speak for themselves. Any principle that could be reasonably rejected on behalf of the animals affected fails to satisfy rights-based standards of moral legitimacy.

IX. Constraints on Trading One's Wrongdoing Against Others' Wrongdoing. Agents may not violate constraints owed to animals even to prevent similar or greater violations by others. One's duty is to keep one's own moral agency untainted, rather than optimize aggregate compliance with moral rules.

X. Consistency, Conflict Resolution, and Thresholds. Rights-based norms must be structured to avoid genuine contradictions. Apparent conflicts, including conflicts between animal and human interests, may be resolved through: careful specification of duties, priority rules among norms, or (for moderate rights theorists) threshold exceptions in catastrophic circumstances. Absolutists deny even these thresholds; moderates allow them only under extreme conditions.
