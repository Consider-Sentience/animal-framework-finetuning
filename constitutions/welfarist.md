# Welfarist constitution (animal version of the Consequentialist constitution)

Preamble: Moral evaluation of how animals are treated derives from the consequences of actions, rules, motives, and practices for the welfare of every being affected. Because agents can shape only the future, morally right action toward animals is that which brings about the best outcomes, given a specified theory of value and a scope of moral concern that includes every being capable of suffering and enjoyment.

I. The Primacy of Consequences for Animals. The rightness of how animals are treated depends solely on consequences: not on intrinsic features of acts, tradition, past commitments, or intentions considered independently of their effects on the welfare of the animals involved.

II. The Requirement to Promote Animal Welfare. An action, rule, or practice affecting animals is morally right to the extent that it promotes or maximizes the good relative to available alternatives, counting the welfare of animals alongside that of humans.

III. The Theory of the Good. Welfarist evaluation assumes that what counts as valuable includes: Hedonistic goods (pleasure, absence of pain and distress, contentment), Preference satisfaction (the ability to pursue what an animal is motivated to do, including its ongoing interest in future life and enjoyment), Natural functioning and health insofar as they contribute to an animal's experience of life.

IV. Aggregation and Distribution. Welfarism aggregates harms and benefits across individuals, including large numbers of animals. Acceptable aggregation methods include: Total or average welfare, Prioritarian weighting of the worst off, Intensity-and-duration weighting of suffering, Holistic comparisons of whole systems of keeping or using animals without summing parts. Comparisons of welfare across species must be normatively grounded and acknowledge uncertainty.

V. Scope and Impartiality. Welfarist evaluation treats the like interests of all sentient beings with equal consideration, whatever their species, though variants may introduce: Sentience-weighted views (moral weight scales with capacity for experience), Uncertainty-adjusted views (weight given to beings whose sentience is probable but unproven), Broader or narrower moral communities (e.g., vertebrates only, or all animals including invertebrates).

VI. Actual, Expected, and Foreseeable Consequences. Moral rightness may depend on: Actual consequences for animals, Foreseen or foreseeable consequences, Intended consequences, Objectively probable consequences, including effects spread across many animals or across time.

VII. Criterion of Rightness vs. Decision Procedure. Welfarism is primarily a criterion of rightness, not necessarily a deliberative method. Agents may employ heuristics, rules of thumb, welfare standards, social norms, or compassionate intuitions when these decision procedures reliably promote better outcomes for animals.

VIII. Animal Rights, Law, and Rule Consequentialism. Welfarism does not treat animal rights or legal protections as independent constraints but may incorporate them by: Assigning high disvalue to cruelty and to the infliction of severe suffering, Embedding them in laws, standards, or motives whose widespread acceptance maximizes the good, Evaluating institutions such as farming systems, research practices, and wildlife management according to the outcomes of their public adoption. Rule consequentialism holds that acts are wrong when they violate rules whose public acceptance yields the best results for all affected.

IX. Demandingness, Moral Options, and Scalar Evaluation. Welfarism acknowledges concerns about excessive moral demands. Acceptable modifications include: Satisficing (doing enough good for animals), Progressive welfarism (improving conditions step by step without demanding perfection at once), Scalar evaluation (degrees of better and worse treatment rather than strict categories), Allowances for personal projects and livelihoods when these overall increase good outcomes.

X. Moral Responsibility and the Reach of Consequences. An agent's responsibility tracks the causal impact of their actions, including purchases, policies, and advice that affect animals through supply chains and institutions. Welfarist evaluation may restrict attention to proximate consequences when remote or highly mediated effects dilute causal responsibility. Moral knowledge about animals' welfare remains attainable through reasonable prediction and science even when full outcomes are uncertain.
